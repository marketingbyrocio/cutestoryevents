]]> An event planning checklist turns a big, exciting, slightly terrifying idea into a series of manageable decisions. Use this timeline as your master guide, then customize it for the size and complexity of your event. A dinner for 30 may need only a few weeks. A conference, festival, fundraiser, or hybrid event can require six months to a year.

The rule is simple: start with the decisions that affect everything else. Your goals, budget, date, venue, and guest count shape nearly every contract and deadline that follows.

Before you start: build the planning foundation

Before counting backward from event day, write down five basics:

  • Purpose: Why are you holding this event?
  • Audience: Who needs to attend, and what do they need from the experience?
  • Outcome: What should guests know, feel, or do afterward?
  • Budget: What can you responsibly spend, including contingency funds?
  • Decision-makers: Who approves contracts, budget changes, messaging, and final details?

Create one shared planning home for the budget, task list, contact information, contracts, meeting notes, and run of show. It can be a spreadsheet, project-management tool, or shared drive. The tool matters less than having one source of truth.

3 to 6 months before the event

For a large conference or festival, start earlier. For a smaller gathering, this phase may happen two or three months out.

Define scope and success

  • Confirm the event goal and target audience.
  • Estimate guest count and establish registration or invitation criteria.
  • Choose in-person, virtual, or hybrid format.
  • Define measurable success indicators, such as registrations, attendance, funds raised, sponsor revenue, leads, survey scores, or community participation.
  • Identify the planning team and decision-making process.

Build the budget

  • List major expense categories: venue, food and beverage, AV, rentals, staffing, entertainment, travel, permits, marketing, accessibility, insurance, and planner fees.
  • Add taxes, service charges, gratuities, delivery, setup, and overtime—not only advertised base prices.
  • Hold back 10% to 15% for contingency when possible.
  • Establish approval thresholds for new expenses.
  • If the event earns revenue, set ticket, sponsor, vendor, or fundraising targets.

Select the date and venue

  • Check organizational calendars, holidays, major local events, school schedules, and likely travel conflicts.
  • Create a venue requirements list before requesting proposals.
  • Tour or virtually review finalists.
  • Compare full costs, capacity by room layout, accessibility, parking, public transit, catering, AV, load-in, and cancellation terms.
  • Review the contract carefully and confirm setup and breakdown hours.
  • Obtain event insurance if required.

Start the event identity and communications plan

  • Confirm the event name, key message, and visual direction.
  • Reserve the URL, registration platform, and social handles if needed.
  • Create a marketing calendar with announcements, reminders, partner promotion, and final information.
  • Draft the save-the-date or early announcement.

Begin vendor, speaker, and sponsor outreach

  • Identify priority vendors and request proposals.
  • Build speaker or performer criteria, invitation language, and deadlines.
  • Create sponsor levels and benefits before outreach.
  • Decide what vendors, sponsors, and speakers must submit: contracts, logos, headshots, bios, slides, certificates, menus, or insurance documents.

1 to 3 months before the event

This is the busiest planning phase. Major partners are confirmed, and the event begins turning from concept into a real guest journey.

Finalize vendors and logistics

  • Sign contracts and enter every payment deadline on the master calendar.
  • Confirm catering menu, service style, dietary process, and guaranteed-count deadline.
  • Book AV, lighting, staging, rentals, transportation, security, entertainment, photography, and other production partners.
  • Share venue rules and load-in instructions with every relevant vendor.
  • Create a contact sheet with names, roles, phone numbers, arrival times, and emergency contacts.

Build the agenda

  • Map the guest experience from arrival through departure.
  • Confirm sessions, speakers, performances, meals, breaks, sponsor moments, and transitions.
  • Add realistic time for movement, restroom breaks, room resets, and late starts.
  • Check that the program supports the event goal instead of merely filling time.
  • For hybrid or virtual events, design specific remote engagement rather than treating online guests as spectators.

Open and manage registration

  • Build the registration form and confirmation email.
  • Collect only information you will actually use.
  • Ask about accessibility needs, dietary requirements, pronouns when relevant, and communication preferences.
  • Test registration on desktop and mobile.
  • Establish a refund, transfer, and waitlist policy.
  • Schedule reminder emails with arrival instructions and deadlines.

Coordinate speakers, sponsors, vendors, and volunteers

  • Send a clear onboarding packet for each group.
  • Track contracts, invoices, assets, arrival times, and open questions.
  • Schedule speaker preparation and technical checks.
  • Confirm sponsor benefits, placements, speaking moments, passes, and deliverables.
  • Recruit volunteers based on actual roles and shift lengths.
  • Assign a single contact for each partner group so nobody receives conflicting information.

Develop the staffing plan

  • List every operational position: event lead, registration, room managers, volunteer lead, speaker manager, vendor contact, stage manager, tech lead, hospitality, accessibility support, runners, and cleanup.
  • Assign decision authority and escalation paths.
  • Create shifts with breaks, meals, and handoffs.
  • Identify what staff members need to know versus what only the event lead needs to manage.

2 to 4 weeks before the event

At this point, the focus shifts from booking to confirmation and execution.

Create the run of show

The run of show is the minute-by-minute operational timeline. Include:

  • Setup and vendor arrivals
  • Staff and volunteer calls
  • Doors open and registration
  • Every program segment and transition
  • AV cues, music, slides, lighting, and microphones
  • Catering service and room resets
  • Speaker locations and stage movements
  • Sponsor acknowledgments
  • Breaks, departures, and breakdown
  • The person responsible for each action

Use a version number and date so everyone knows which document is current.

Conduct final confirmations

  • Confirm every vendor’s arrival, contact, scope, balance, and requirements.
  • Send speakers the final agenda, arrival instructions, and presentation deadlines.
  • Verify sponsor deliverables and onsite needs.
  • Confirm volunteer shifts and training.
  • Review accessibility requests and assign responsibility for each accommodation.
  • Recheck permits, insurance, security, alcohol service, and emergency plans.

Build the floor plan and signage list

  • Map registration, seating, stage, food, bars, sponsor areas, vendors, activities, quiet space, and accessible routes.
  • Check line capacity and crowd flow.
  • Create a list of every sign, its exact wording, size, and placement.
  • Plan directional signs from parking or transit to the entrance.
  • Add reserved seating and accessibility signs where appropriate.

Prepare supplies and production files

  • Print badges, lists, schedules, table signs, cue sheets, and backup copies.
  • Collect slide decks and test them in the correct format.
  • Build an event kit with tape, scissors, markers, chargers, adapters, batteries, first-aid basics, stain remover, zip ties, and other useful tiny heroes.
  • Prepare petty cash or purchasing procedures if staff may need emergency supplies.

The week of the event

  • Hold a final team meeting and review the run of show.
  • Walk through the venue with the operational leads.
  • Send guests the final arrival email.
  • Check the weather and activate the backup plan if necessary.
  • Confirm final catering numbers and dietary accommodations.
  • Test registration, Wi-Fi, presentation files, livestream links, captions, microphones, and devices.
  • Pack supplies by function so setup is not a treasure hunt.
  • Print offline copies of essential contacts and timelines.
  • Sleep. Seriously. Exhaustion is not an event-planning strategy.

Event-day checklist

Before doors open

  • Arrive before vendors and verify venue access.
  • Walk the space for cleanliness, safety, temperature, lighting, and damage.
  • Check every room against the floor plan.
  • Test AV, microphones, videos, slides, Wi-Fi, captions, and streaming.
  • Brief staff and volunteers on roles, breaks, communications, and emergencies.
  • Confirm speaker arrivals and green-room needs.
  • Check registration materials and guest lists.
  • Verify food labels, dietary meals, and service timing.
  • Remove clutter, packaging, and empty cases from guest areas.

During the event

  • Keep the run of show current and communicate changes quickly.
  • Watch the guest experience, not only the schedule.
  • Monitor room capacity, lines, temperature, sound, food, and accessibility.
  • Give speakers and vendors advance cues.
  • Document issues and decisions for follow-up.
  • Protect the client from unnecessary interruptions; bring them only decisions they need to make.
  • Stay calm. Guests take their emotional cue from the team.

At closing and breakdown

  • Announce final transportation or departure information.
  • Secure gifts, cash, technology, signage, and client property.
  • Confirm vendor pickup and rental counts.
  • Walk the venue with the manager if required.
  • Photograph any damage or unresolved issue.
  • Check that staff and volunteers have safe transportation home.

After the event

The event is not finished when the last guest leaves.

  • Send thank-you messages to guests, speakers, sponsors, vendors, volunteers, and partners.
  • Distribute the feedback survey while the experience is fresh.
  • Reconcile invoices, tips, reimbursements, ticket revenue, and donations.
  • Deliver photos, recordings, resources, or promised follow-up materials.
  • Report sponsor results and fulfillment.
  • Hold a team debrief: what worked, what changed, and what should be different next time?
  • Save final files, contracts, notes, and vendor information in an organized archive.
  • Compare actual results with the success measures established at the beginning.

How to make this checklist work for you

Every item needs an owner and a deadline. “Confirm catering” is not a plan until one person is responsible for doing it by a specific date. Review the checklist weekly at first, then more frequently as the event approaches.

If the list feels like a second full-time job, that is useful information. You may not need full-service planning, but you probably need more staffing, a narrower scope, or professional support for the highest-risk areas. ]]> An event planner turns an event goal into a workable plan, then coordinates the people, money, deadlines, and details needed to deliver it. Before the event, that can include budgeting, venue research, vendor and sponsor management, speaker onboarding, registration, agenda development, and timelines. During the event, the planner manages setup, staff, vendors, program flow, and surprises. Afterward, they handle wrap-up, feedback, invoicing, and lessons for the next event.

In other words: the planner makes sure somebody is thinking about the whole event while everyone else focuses on their part.

Event planner vs. coordinator vs. day-of manager

These titles overlap, and different companies use them differently. The safest approach is to ignore the title and read the scope.

An event planner is usually involved early and helps shape the budget, venue, vendors, program, communications, and execution plan. A coordinator often takes an existing plan and manages details, confirmations, and logistics. A day-of manager or on-site manager leads execution at the venue.

Even “day-of” service requires preparation before event day. The manager must review contracts, learn the timeline, confirm vendors, understand the floor plan, and know who can make decisions. Nobody can responsibly walk in at sunrise with no context and run a complex event by lunch.

What an event planner does before the event

Most event-planning work happens before guests arrive.

Clarifies goals and scope

The planner asks why the event exists, who it serves, what success looks like, and what the organization can spend. Those answers guide every later choice.

They also define what is—and is not—included. A clear scope protects the budget and prevents important work from falling into the space between team members.

Builds and manages the budget

An event budget includes far more than venue rent and catering. Planners track labor, AV, rentals, transportation, permits, insurance, accessibility, printing, gifts, taxes, service charges, tips, delivery, travel, and contingency funds.

They compare estimates with actual and committed costs, flag risk early, and help the client make tradeoffs while choices are still available.

Finds and contracts the venue

Venue research includes capacity, room layouts, guest flow, accessibility, vendor access, parking, transit, technology, catering rules, cost, and contract terms. A planner gathers proposals, schedules tours, compares options, and helps negotiate the agreement.

The goal is not simply to find a pretty room. It is to find a space where the program can actually work.

Researches and manages vendors

Planners identify vendors, request proposals, compare scope and cost, review contracts, track payments, and keep partners informed. Depending on the event, this may include catering, AV, rentals, decor, entertainment, photography, security, transportation, printing, and staffing.

Vendor management continues after booking. The planner confirms arrival times, access instructions, floor plans, equipment, dietary information, contacts, and final changes.

Develops the agenda and guest experience

The agenda is more than a list of sessions. The planner considers arrival, check-in, transitions, movement, breaks, food, sponsor visibility, networking, accessibility, and departure.

For conferences and virtual events, they may also manage speaker invitations, biographies, headshots, slide deadlines, tech rehearsals, travel, and green-room needs.

Coordinates sponsors and exhibitors

Sponsor management can include prospect tracking, proposals, benefit fulfillment, contracts, invoices, logos, ads, passes, speaking moments, booth requirements, and reporting. The planner keeps the sponsor promise aligned with what the event team can realistically deliver.

Manages registration and communications

Planners help select registration tools, build forms, manage confirmation emails, track attendance, handle guest questions, and prepare check-in. They coordinate information across guests, staff, vendors, speakers, volunteers, sponsors, and the venue so each group knows what to do.

Creates the master timeline and run of show

The planning timeline tracks months of work. The run of show tracks the event minute by minute. It includes setup, staff calls, doors, program segments, transitions, AV cues, catering, room resets, and breakdown.

Every meaningful action needs an owner. The planner identifies dependencies, updates the documents as decisions change, and makes sure the team is working from the current version.

What an event planner does during the event

On-site, the planner becomes the event’s operational center.

They oversee venue access, vendor arrivals, room setup, registration, staff and volunteer briefings, speaker readiness, catering, technology, signage, accessibility, and schedule. They cue transitions, solve problems, and communicate changes without creating unnecessary panic.

Good planners are constantly scanning. Is a line getting too long? Is the room temperature uncomfortable? Has a speaker arrived? Can wheelchair users move through the updated floor plan? Is catering ready for the next break? Does the client need to know about the issue—or can the team fix it quietly?

Unexpected things happen even at well-planned events. A vendor is delayed, the microphone stops working, a speaker changes slides, weather shifts, or a guest needs support. The planner evaluates the impact, activates the backup plan, and keeps the experience moving.

That on-the-fly problem solving is not separate from planning. It is the reason the detailed plans exist: when one variable changes, the planner understands what else it affects.

What an event planner does after the event

After guests leave, the planner may:

  • Oversee breakdown, rentals, and final venue checks
  • Reconcile invoices, reimbursements, revenue, or donations
  • Confirm sponsor benefits and reporting
  • Send thank-yous and feedback surveys
  • Organize photos, recordings, attendee resources, or follow-up emails
  • Review attendance, survey results, leads, funds raised, or other success measures
  • Lead a debrief and document improvements
  • Close contracts and archive final files

This phase protects relationships and turns one event into useful knowledge for the next.

What does an event planner do every day?

There is no perfectly typical day. During early planning, the work may be research, budgets, proposals, and strategy. In the middle, it shifts toward contracts, meetings, onboarding, and timelines. Close to the event, the days fill with confirmations, rehearsals, production documents, and contingency planning.

A planner might spend one morning comparing venue contracts, the afternoon leading a speaker meeting, and the evening revising a run of show after a client decision. Much of the job is communication: collecting accurate information, translating it for the right people, and following up until decisions become action.

When should you hire an event planner?

Professional support is especially valuable when:

  • The event affects your revenue, reputation, donors, employees, or community
  • You are planning outside your experience
  • The event has many vendors, speakers, sponsors, volunteers, or locations
  • Your team does not have enough time to own the logistics
  • You need someone neutral to coordinate multiple stakeholders
  • You want to participate in the event instead of managing it
  • A failure would cost more than the planning support

You do not always need full-service planning. You may need venue research, a timeline, speaker or sponsor management, an event audit, or an experienced on-site lead. A good planner helps define the right level of support.
